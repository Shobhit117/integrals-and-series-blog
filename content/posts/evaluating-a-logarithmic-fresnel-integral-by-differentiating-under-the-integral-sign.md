Title: Evaluating a logarithmic Fresnel integral by differentiating under the integral sign

Author: Shobhit Bhatnagar
Category: Feynman's Trick

Summary: Differentiating the Mellin transform of the sine function with respect to its exponent evaluates a logarithmic Fresnel-type integral in terms of Euler's constant.
Status: published

A factor of $\ln x$ in an integrand can often be generated by differentiating a family of power integrals $x^{s-1}$ with respect to the exponent $s$. Combining this principle with the Mellin transform of $\sin t$ proved in [an earlier post]({filename}/posts/mellin-transforms-of-sine-and-cosine-via-a-quarter-circle-contour.md), we will show that

$$
\int_0^\infty \frac{\sin\left(x^2\right)\ln(x)}{x}\,dx = -\frac{\gamma \pi}{8},
$$

where $\gamma$ is the Euler-Mascheroni constant.

## Reducing to a Mellin derivative

First, the substitution $t = x^2$ (so that $\frac{dx}{x} = \frac{dt}{2t}$ and $\ln x = \frac{1}{2}\ln t$) removes the quadratic argument inside the sine:

$$
\int_0^\infty \frac{\sin\left(x^2\right)\ln(x)}{x}\,dx = \frac{1}{4}\int_0^\infty \frac{\sin(t) \ln(t)}{t}\,dt. \tag{1}
$$

Recall from [our post on the Mellin transforms of sine and cosine]({filename}/posts/mellin-transforms-of-sine-and-cosine-via-a-quarter-circle-contour.md) that for $s \in (-1, 1) \setminus \{0\}$,

$$
\int_0^\infty t^{s-1}\sin(t)\,dt = \sin\left(\frac{\pi s}{2}\right)\Gamma(s),
$$

with removable singularity at $s = 0$ matching the Dirichlet integral $\frac{\pi}{2}$. Differentiating under the integral sign with respect to $s$ at $s = 0$ produces the logarithmic factor $\ln t$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_0^\infty \frac{\sin(t)\ln(t)}{t}\,dt
&= \lim_{s\to 0}\frac{d}{ds}\int_0^\infty t^{s-1} \sin(t)\,dt \\
&= \lim_{s\to 0} \frac{d}{ds}\left[\sin\left(\frac{\pi s}{2}\right) \Gamma(s)\right] \\
&= \lim_{s\to 0} \left[ \frac{\pi}{2}\cos\left(\frac{\pi s}{2}\right)\Gamma(s) + \sin\left(\frac{\pi s}{2}\right)\Gamma'(s)\right].
\end{aligned}
$$

## Taking the limit via Laurent expansions

Near $s = 0$, the gamma function and its derivative have the Laurent expansions

$$
\Gamma(s) = \frac{1}{s} - \gamma + O(s),
\qquad
\Gamma'(s) = -\frac{1}{s^2} + \frac{6\gamma^2+\pi^2}{12} + O(s).
$$

Substituting these into the limit gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_0^\infty \frac{\sin(t)\ln(t)}{t}\,dt
&= \lim_{s\to 0} \left[ \frac{\pi}{2}\cos\left(\frac{\pi s}{2}\right)\left(\frac{1}{s}-\gamma+O(s) \right) + \sin\left(\frac{\pi s}{2}\right)\left(-\frac{1}{s^2}+\frac{6\gamma^2+\pi^2}{12}+O(s) \right) \right] \\
&= \lim_{s\to 0} \left[-\frac{\pi \gamma}{2} + \left( \frac{\pi}{2}\cos\left(\frac{\pi s}{2}\right) \frac{1}{s} - \sin\left(\frac{\pi s}{2}\right)\frac{1}{s^2}\right) + O(s)\right].
\end{aligned}
$$

Using the Taylor series $\cos\left(\frac{\pi s}{2}\right) = 1 - O(s^2)$ and $\sin\left(\frac{\pi s}{2}\right) = \frac{\pi s}{2} - O(s^3)$, the singular $1/s$ terms cancel identically:

$$
\frac{\pi}{2}\cos\left(\frac{\pi s}{2}\right) \frac{1}{s} - \sin\left(\frac{\pi s}{2}\right)\frac{1}{s^2} = \frac{\pi}{2s} - \frac{\pi}{2s} + O(s) = O(s).
$$

Therefore,

$$
\int_0^\infty \frac{\sin(t)\ln(t)}{t}\,dt = -\frac{\pi \gamma}{2}. \tag{2}
$$

### A shortcut via $\Gamma(s+1) = s\Gamma(s)$

We can also see why the singular terms cancel without writing out the second-order Laurent coefficients by factoring $\sin\left(\frac{\pi s}{2}\right)\Gamma(s)$ as

$$
\sin\left(\frac{\pi s}{2}\right)\Gamma(s) = \frac{\sin\left(\frac{\pi s}{2}\right)}{s}\Gamma(1+s).
$$

Here $g(s) = \frac{\sin(\pi s/2)}{s}$ is a smooth, even function with $g(0) = \frac{\pi}{2}$ and $g'(0) = 0$, while $\Gamma(1) = 1$ and $\Gamma'(1) = -\gamma$. Applying the product rule at $s=0$ immediately yields $g'(0)\Gamma(1) + g(0)\Gamma'(1) = -\frac{\pi\gamma}{2}$.

Substituting equation (2) into equation (1) completes the proof:

$$
\int_0^\infty \frac{\sin\left(x^2\right)\ln(x)}{x}\,dx = -\frac{\gamma \pi}{8}.
$$
